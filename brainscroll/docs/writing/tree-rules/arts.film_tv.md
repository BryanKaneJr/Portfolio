# Film & TV: tree rules

- Settled history and craft only (CURRENT_PRODUCT_DECISIONS §20). No recent box office, no "best ever" rankings, no poll results, no current franchises or their characters. When money shows why something mattered (Jaws, Snow White, VHS rentals), use a dated figure from an authoritative source, or better, describe the change without a number. Name films as examples of a technique or a turning point, never as a verdict on taste.
- Level art never shows film stills, characters, logos or real people. Cards may name directors and actors and describe what they did; they never quote dialogue or screenplays except from public-domain works (for example silent-era intertitles), and never reproduce lyrics.
- "Firsts" and legends are where writers go wrong. Say "one of the first" or "popularized" unless a first is well documented. Griffith refined and popularized close-ups and cross-cutting; he did not invent them. The crowd fleeing the Lumières' train is a legend, and the Kinetoscope was developed largely by W. K. L. Dickson in Edison's lab. Explain the illusion of motion with flicker fusion and apparent motion, and call "persistence of vision" the older, oversimplified explanation.
- Race, representation and politics are stated plainly and even-handedly: The Birth of a Nation's glorification of the Ku Klux Klan and the protests against it, blackface in The Jazz Singer, Hollywood's picture of Native Americans in Westerns, the staging in Nanook of the North. For the blacklist, explain what the House Un-American Activities Committee did and argued, what those blacklisted and their defenders argued, and how it ended; no verdict on present-day politics.
- Sources: Library of Congress (National Film Registry essays, National Screening Room), the Academy's Margaret Herrick Library and Academy Museum, the American Film Institute Catalog, BFI, MoMA, Museum of the Moving Image, Smithsonian, Encyclopaedia Britannica, the Paley Center and the Television Academy Foundation's archive for TV, and the FCC or Congress.gov for broadcast law (Public Broadcasting Act of 1967). Court cases (Paramount 1948, Betamax 1984) from the decisions themselves or Oyez. Studio or streaming company pages only for their own founding dates.
- Streaming, cable and digital levels teach how the change happened and why, dated to its years; no subscriber counts, current service rankings or claims about which company is winning.
- Overlaps. Everyday Technology owns how cameras, sensors, TV signals and screens work (its Levels 44 to 47): refer to them in a phrase and teach what filmmakers do with them. Music owns film scores as music (its Level 94, "Film Music") and music videos: Level 94 here is the viewer's side (spotting and testing how a score steers you), so do not re-teach leitmotifs or composers. Art History owns perspective, photography's arrival and composition in painting: call back to it, don't repeat it. Psychology owns perception and the 1957 subliminal popcorn hoax: don't retell it. Literature owns written stories and story structure (no hero's journey level); Level 97 approaches adaptation from the film side. Logic owns framing effects in general; the documentary levels stay with filmmaking choices.
- Keep chapters in their lanes: Chapter 2 tells Eisenstein's montage as history, Chapter 4 teaches the Kuleshov effect as a mechanism; Chapter 3 introduces lenses, Level 46 applies deep focus to Citizen Kane; Chapter 4 explains editing and sound, Chapter 10 asks how they steer a viewer. Level 19 introduces documentary history, Levels 95 and 96 analyze it.
- World cinema is told with respect and on its own terms: name specific films, places and makers; no "exotic" framing and no ranking of national cinemas against Hollywood. Use native names with correct diacritics (Méliès, Yasujirō Ozu).
