# Literature: tree rules (living traditions, public-domain quotes)
- Myth is used in the scholarly sense: a traditional sacred story, never a synonym for "false". Stories that are still sacred to living communities (the Mahabharata and Ramayana for Hindus, the Kojiki's gods in Shinto, the Popol Vuh for K'iche' Maya people, Native nations' creation stories, Anansi and Sundiata for West African communities) are taught with respect, attributed to the people who tell them ("the K'iche' Maya tell ..."), and never ranked or mocked. Say "Hindus revere the Bhagavad Gita" and teach it here only as part of the Mahabharata's story; World Religions owns belief, practice and scripture as religion. Do not call the Bible or the Quran myth. Level 4 states the Genesis parallel as scholars describe it, with no verdict on either text's truth.
- Quotes: verbatim only from public-domain texts. In the US, works first published in 1930 or earlier are public domain in 2026; later works (Hurston's 1937 novel, Orwell, Huxley, Tolkien, Achebe, García Márquez, Morrison, most Hughes after 1930) are paraphrased, never quoted. A translation has its own copyright: quote an ancient or foreign work only from a public-domain translation (for example Samuel Butler's Homer, Longfellow's Dante, the Constance Garnett Tolstoy and Dostoevsky, Burton or Lane for the Nights) and name the translator; otherwise paraphrase. Check every quote's wording against Project Gutenberg, the Folger Shakespeare Library or the Library of Congress. Never invent or "tidy" a quote.
- The no-em-dash rule applies to quotes on cards too. Dickinson's dashes (Level 76) are described in words; quote only dash-free lines of hers, or put the full line in a `supportingQuote`. Write titles plainly (no italics markup).
- Settled history and craft only, as with the other media trees: no recent bestsellers, no "greatest books" lists or rankings, no sales figures, no living authors as the subject of a level. Prizes appear only as dated facts where they are the point (Tagore's 1913 Nobel Prize in Literature, Level 87). Film & TV owns adaptations and screen storytelling: mention a film version in a phrase at most, never as a card's subject. Philosophy owns philosophers: Aristotle appears only for the Poetics (Level 39), Kafka and Dostoevsky are taught as novelists, not existentialism.
- Dates for ancient and medieval works are estimates: say "about" and give the range sources give (the earliest Gilgamesh poems, Homer, the Mahabharata, Beowulf's composition). "Homer" names a tradition as much as a person; authorship is debated. Attributions ("often called the first novel" for Genji and Don Quixote, "first recorded in Shakespeare" rather than "invented by Shakespeare" for Level 55, per the Oxford English Dictionary) must carry their hedge. Aladdin and Ali Baba were added to the Nights by the French translator Antoine Galland in the 1700s (from the Syrian storyteller Hanna Diyab); say so if they come up.
- Sensitivities. Slavery (Levels 74, 77, 79) follows US History's terms: "enslaved people", violence stated plainly and briefly. Never print the racial slur in Huckleberry Finn; describe the debate over teaching it as a debate between named sides (those who read it as an anti-racist satire, those who find its language and portrayal of Jim harmful), without a verdict. Br'er Rabbit tales come from African American oral tradition; Joel Chandler Harris, a white journalist, published them, and his plantation framing is criticized. Colonialism in Level 88 is told from Achebe's stated aims. Murder, war and Gothic horror (Levels 62, 68, 82) stay unexplicit. The Shakespeare authorship question (Level 98) is a fringe theory mainstream scholars reject; say so plainly.
- Sources: Britannica, the Poetry Foundation and the Academy of American Poets (poets.org) for poets and forms, the Folger Shakespeare Library and the Shakespeare Birthplace Trust, the British Library (Beowulf, the Canterbury Tales manuscripts, Gothic and Romantic collections), the Library of Congress, the Smithsonian (NMAAHC, National Museum of the American Indian, National Museum of Asian Art), the British Museum and the Penn Museum (Gilgamesh and the flood tablet), the Morgan Library, NobelPrize.org for prizes, university presses and named scholars for interpretations. Project Gutenberg for public-domain text wording only. Wikipedia for discovery only.
- Overlaps; do not contradict or repeat them. Ancient Greece: Level 6 (Homer's Iliad and Odyssey), 41 to 49 (the Olympians, Heracles, Perseus, Theseus, Odysseus's voyage), 71 to 75 (theaters, tragedy and its great writers, comedy, Sappho), 95 (myths on screen). Here, teach only how Homer was composed (Level 6), epic conventions (38), Aristotle's terms (39) and Antigone as a play (41); recall plots and names in a phrase. Ancient Rome Level 47 owns the Aeneid (a phrase in Level 38). Ancient Egypt Levels 31 to 37 own Egypt's gods and the Book of the Dead (a phrase at most in Levels 5 and 19). The Middle Ages owns the Vikings (13 to 15), monasteries copying books (7), papermaking (65) and the Arthur and Robin Hood legends (78): Levels 11 to 13 and 47 teach the Eddas, sagas and Beowulf as texts, not Viking history. Everyday Technology Level 41 owns how the printing press works: Level 57 is about readers and the novel. US History owns Douglass as an abolitionist and orator (46, 92) and the Great Migration and Harlem's rise (73): Levels 74 and 78 teach the books and poems. Art History owns Romanticism, Realism and Surrealism in painting (51, 54, 69) and Symbols in Art (93); Music owns troubadours and opera. Callbacks in a phrase are welcome.
- Within the tree: Level 37 teaches free verse as a form (with Whitman as one example); Level 75 teaches Whitman's book and vision, recalling 37. Level 29 teaches genre in general; Chapter 10 teaches particular genres. Level 7's Ovid is recalled by Level 53 (Pyramus and Thisbe behind Romeo and Juliet) and Level 85 (Kafka's echo of the title); Level 63 recalls the Prometheus myth; Level 96 recalls Levels 11, 12 and 47. Chapters 7 and 8 must make those connections; Chapters 9 and 10 ask the learner to weigh readings, compare versions or apply a tool to a new passage, and should never turn into more author biographies.
