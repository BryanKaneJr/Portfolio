# US History: tree rules (balance and inclusion)
- Scope is the first Americans to about 2000. Chronology runs through Chapter 9 (to Watergate, about 1975); Chapter 10 carries the 1980s to 1991 only inside Level 97 (why the Cold War ended). The only later event is September 11, 2001 (Level 98), told factually. Nothing after 2001: no later presidents, elections, court cases, removals of monuments or current controversies, even as "today" examples. A level may say an institution still exists (Social Security, tribal governments, Title IX) but gives no post-2001 events or figures, except a stable count said with "about" or "more than" (e.g. "more than 570 federally recognized tribes").
- Balanced, not neutral about facts. Where Americans still disagree (imperialism in 1898, joining World War I, the Gilded Age tycoons, Prohibition, the New Deal, the atomic bomb, Vietnam, the ERA, why the Cold War ended), teach what happened, then what each side argued, attributed to named people, groups or historians, never the app's verdict. Balance applies only where historians genuinely disagree. Where the evidence is settled, say so plainly: slavery was the central cause of secession (the seceding states' own declarations say so); the Lost Cause and the Dunning School are taught as interpretations historians have since rejected, with the evidence, not as one side of a live debate. No partisan framing of any modern party or politician.
- Native nations, enslaved and free Black Americans, women and immigrants are the main story, not sidebars: name people and nations (Wampanoag, Cherokee, Lakota; Metacom, Sacagawea, Tecumseh), give them agency (alliances, lawsuits, revolts, escapes, activism), and use their own records where they exist. Use "enslaved people" (not "slaves" as a noun for people), "Native nations" or the nation's own name ("Indian" only in historical names such as the Indian Removal Act), "incarceration" for the Japanese American camps (naming "internment" as the older official term). Violence (the Middle Passage, removal, massacres, lynching) is stated plainly and briefly, never dwelt on and never softened.
- Numbers that are estimates (pre-1492 populations, first arrival dates, Middle Passage totals, Trail of Tears deaths, war dead) are given as rounded figures or ranges with "about" or "estimates range from", naming the source (e.g. the SlaveVoyages database for the slave trade). Never invent quotes: verbatim quotes only from public-domain texts (founding documents, Douglass, Lincoln, the Declaration of Sentiments, federal records), and paraphrase anything still in copyright (e.g. Martin Luther King Jr.'s writings).
- Sources: the National Archives and its founding documents, the Library of Congress, the National Park Service, the Smithsonian (NMAH, NMAAHC, National Museum of the American Indian), the US House and Senate historians, the Office of the Historian (State Department), the Gilder Lehrman Institute, Britannica, university presses and named historians for interpretations. Tribal nations' own official sites are authoritative for their histories. Wikipedia for discovery only.
- Myths are flagged as myths (the 1621 "first Thanksgiving" story, Pocahontas saving Smith, Betsy Ross's flag, Washington's cherry tree, Paul Revere's lone ride). Level 99 debunks a few; elsewhere, just tell the record and note the legend in a phrase.
- Chapter 10 is the reasoning chapter. Each level is built around a question: what the evidence is, how historians have explained it, and how to weigh the explanations. Only Levels 97 (the 1980s to 1991) and 98 (9/11) teach events no earlier level covers; the rest recall earlier chapters (Level 93 recalls Chapters 5 and 6, 94 recalls Level 59, 95 recalls Level 76, 96 recalls Level 78). Do not turn it into more chronology or re-teach those events.
- Overlaps. How Government Works owns how the Constitution, Congress, courts, elections and voting rights work in general (its Level 32 already covers the Constitution's seven articles, ratification by nine states and the Bill of Rights; Level 37 the amendments that widened the vote; Level 38 women's suffrage abroad; Level 39 Rosa Parks, the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Selma, so Levels 83 and 84 teach Brown, Little Rock, sit-ins, Freedom Rides and the March on Washington and recall the 1964 law and Selma in a phrase). Here, teach the making and the people: the arguments at Philadelphia, the campaigns and strategies, not how the institutions run, and recall those levels in a phrase rather than re-teaching them. How Money Works owns the Depression's economic causes (Level 69), unions and collective bargaining (87), central banks (55), safety nets (59) and Keynes (93): here, Levels 65, 75, 76 and 95 teach lived experience, events and the political fight, not economic mechanisms. The Middle Ages owns Columbus's 1492 voyage (94) and the Maya, Aztec and Inca (97): here, start from North America and the Columbian Exchange. Do not contradict any of them.
- Keep US spelling and US terms (fall, colonists, the Revolution). Dates in full for anything learners are tested on ("1787"); days only when they are the point (July 4, 1776; September 11, 2001).
