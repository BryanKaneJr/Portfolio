# Logic & Critical Thinking: tree rules (neutral examples, reasoning not psychology)

- **Politically neutral examples, always.** Never illustrate a fallacy, bias or rhetorical trick with a real current party, politician, movement, election or hot-button issue (abortion, guns, immigration, climate policy, vaccine mandates, elections, wars in the news), and never imply that one side reasons worse than another. Use everyday cases (pizza toppings, sports fans, siblings, a school board choosing a mascot, ads for toothpaste), invented characters with plain first names, or settled history. Level 53's switch-sides test and Level 88's echo chambers must apply to everyone, including the learner.
- **Teach the error, not a verdict on people.** A fallacy is a flaw in an argument, not proof its conclusion is false (Level 98 teaches the "fallacy fallacy"). Say so wherever it matters: a slippery slope can be real (Level 25), experts can be right to rely on (Level 81), conflicts of interest don't make a claim false (Level 82), some conspiracies were real (Level 86). Never mock believers in pseudoscience, conspiracy claims or myths; show the test and let it do the work.
- **Sourcing.** Authoritative: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy (logic, fallacies, induction, Popper, Occam), Britannica, open logic textbooks (forall x, from universities), Purdue OWL (fallacies, rhetoric), UC Berkeley's Understanding Science (how science works), NIH/NCBI, the NHS, Cochrane and the FDA for trials, placebos and blinding, the Royal Society and national academies for peer review and replication. Name coinages and attributions carefully: "no true Scotsman" (Antony Flew), "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" (popularized by Carl Sagan), Goodhart's law (Charles Goodhart; the "when a measure becomes a target" wording is Marilyn Strathern's), Occam's razor (the famous wording is not William of Ockham's own), Doll and Hill's smoking studies and Bradford Hill's criteria (Level 66).
- **Stories that are shakier than they sound.** Abraham Wald and the returning bombers (Level 37) is real, but the famous red-dot diagram is a modern illustration: don't present it as his. The "cobra effect" in colonial Delhi is poorly documented: if used at all, call it a story. Europeans' "all swans are white" before black swans were recorded in Australia (1697) is fine. Don't invent quotes for Aristotle, Popper or Sagan.
- **Psychology owns the mind; Logic owns the reasoning.** Chapter 6 names confirmation bias, motivated reasoning, double standards, patterns in noise and the bias blind spot as *errors in reasoning*, alongside the appeals to nature and tradition, the perfect-solution fallacy and mistaking confidence for evidence, and teaches a habit that counters each. Do not retell the experiments behind them (Tversky and Kahneman's studies, the Linda problem, the wheel of fortune), explain brain mechanisms, or teach "System 1 and System 2" as a model: that is the Psychology tree's material. One clause naming who studied it is fine.
- **Probability & Statistics owns the numbers.** Coincidence (Level 46), calibration (Level 95) and updating beliefs (Level 96) are taught conceptually, with no probability formulas, Bayes' theorem calculations, sampling math, averages or graph-reading. Hasty generalization (35) and survivorship bias (37) are about the reasoning error, not sampling methods. Don't teach the gambler's fallacy or regression to the mean: they belong to Probability & Statistics.
- **Overlaps: build on, don't repeat or contradict.** How Government Works owns "Spotting Misinformation" (69), "Reading the News Well" (95) and "How to Disagree Well" (99): don't reteach lateral reading or news-literacy checklists; Chapter 9 is about reasoning over sources (expertise, consensus, conflicts of interest, primary sources). How Money Works owns opportunity cost (15), scams (19), incentives (48) and advertising (89): Level 49 (Goodhart) and Level 82 (who benefits) may call back to incentives in a phrase, and Chapter 8 teaches the language of persuasion, not how ads drive buying. Ancient Greece owns Socrates (54) and Aristotle's life (57): Level 14 names Aristotle's syllogism and Level 71 his three appeals, nothing more. The Human Body owns clinical trial phases (96): Chapter 7 teaches how science reasons (hypotheses, theories, falsifiability, ad hoc rescues, changing its mind), not how trials are designed. Chemistry's "Chemistry Myths" (99) is its own; don't reuse its "chemical-free" example.
- **Near neighbours inside this tree.** Keep these apart: 17 induction (what it is) vs 35 hasty generalization (the error); 18 black swan (the logic of counterexamples) vs 62 falsifiability (Popper's test for science); 42 correlation vs 44 confounders; 62 falsifiability vs 65 ad hoc rescues (dodging falsification); 46 coincidence (enough chances) vs 57 Texas sharpshooter (target drawn after); 23 straw man vs 97 steel man (its positive twin); 38 extraordinary claims vs 85 Occam's razor vs 86 conspiracy claims; 72 appeals to emotion vs 79 manipulation.
- **Examples that are safe and current.** Illustrate fallacies with invented arguments; if you quote a real ad or headline, make it old and unremarkable. For Level 89 (photos out of context), AI-generated images may be mentioned as a 2026 reality in general terms, with no real incident tied to a political dispute. Health examples stay settled (smoking and lung cancer, handwashing, placebo pills), never contested current treatments.
- **Write for adults and teens.** Formal terms (modus ponens, affirming the consequent, ad hominem) are taught once, with a plain-English name first; after that use the plain name. Arguments in cards are short enough to hold in your head: two or three premises at most.

## Who owns what in Mind & Reasoning (settled across the three trees; overrides anything above)
- **Probability & Statistics owns experimental design and every number:** observational studies vs. experiments, control groups, random assignment, placebos and blinding, significance, replication statistics, base rates and false positives, the gambler's fallacy, regression to the mean, the conjunction rule as arithmetic. Logic and Psychology mention these in a phrase at most, never as a level's concept or a question.
- **Psychology owns the experiments behind biases and heuristics:** availability, anchoring, representativeness (the Linda problem), the halo effect, Wason's 2-4-6 task, overconfidence, and the replication crisis as the history of the field. Logic never retells these experiments.
- **Logic owns reasoning as a skill:** arguments, fallacies (including appeals to nature and tradition, the perfect-solution fallacy), confirmation bias and motivated reasoning taught as habits to counter (no experiments), survivorship bias (Logic 37), Goodhart's law and incentives (Logic 49), coincidence and patterns in noise without arithmetic, how science reasons (falsifiability, hypotheses, theories, ad hoc rescues, how science changes its mind, peer review, pseudoscience), sources and decisions.
- **Elsewhere:** How Money Works owns loss aversion and behavioral economics (Money 95), opportunity cost, incentives in markets and pricing tricks; The Human Body owns the brain, senses and sleep as biology and clinical trial phases; How Government Works owns polls in elections (Government 66) and media literacy (69, 95).
- Callbacks between the three Mind trees are welcome as a phrase ("Logic's straw man"), but no question may depend on another tree.
