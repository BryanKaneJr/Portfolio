# Philosophy: tree rules (positions belong to thinkers, never to the app)

- **Never endorse a position.** Every view is held by a named thinker or school ("Mill argued", "Buddhist philosophers hold", "compatibilists reply"), and every level that presents a view gives at least one serious objection or rival view in the thinker's own terms. Never write "the answer is", "philosophers now agree" or "this was refuted" unless it is plain history (Gettier's cases did change how epistemologists define knowledge; that is a fact about the field, not a verdict). Questions test what a thinker held and why, never which view is correct. In Chapters 9 and 10, "weigh" means lay out the strongest case on each side, not pick a winner.
- **Religion only as philosophers engaged it.** Levels 8, 12 to 14, 42, 75 to 78 and 96 present arguments made by philosophers, with the replies, never as claims about which religion is true. Describe the Buddha, Nagasena, Aquinas, al-Ghazali and Maimonides as thinkers arguing a case; don't teach doctrine, practice, scripture or history of a faith (the World Religions sibling tree owns those). Level 8 is the no-self argument, not Buddhism; Level 9 names schools and one idea each, not Hindu, Jain or Buddhist religion. Arguments for God (75 to 77) and the problem of evil (78) get equal care on both sides; say "theists reply" and "critics reply". "God is dead" (96) is Nietzsche's cultural diagnosis in The Gay Science, not a claim the app makes.
- **Across traditions, with equal respect.** Chinese (Confucius, Laozi, Zhuangzi, Mencius, Xunzi, Mozi, Han Feizi, Wang Yangming), Indian (Nyaya, Vedanta, Carvaka, Jain, Buddhist), Islamic (Avicenna, al-Ghazali, Ibn Rushd), Jewish (Maimonides) and African (ubuntu) thought are philosophy, not "wisdom" or "mysticism". Give names in their usual English forms with the original once where it helps (Avicenna, Ibn Sina). Don't frame non-Western thinkers as anticipating Western ones. Ubuntu (69) is associated with southern African Nguni languages; attribute modern statements of it to named writers (such as Desmond Tutu or Mogobe Ramose) rather than to "Africans".
- **Sensitive topics.** Death (93) and meaning (97, 98) follow safe messaging: Camus's line that suicide is the one serious philosophical problem may be quoted once, with his answer (revolt and living on), no method detail and no romanticizing; don't dwell on it beyond Level 97. Trolley and footbridge cases (81, 82) stay bloodless. No level takes a side on abortion, euthanasia, the death penalty, a current war, a party or a politician; Thomson's violinist is out of scope. Marx (56), Nozick (59), Rawls (58), Machiavelli (53) and Beauvoir (57) are stated as their arguments with a named critic's reply; don't link any of them to a current party or movement. Beauvoir and Wollstonecraft are taught as arguments about women's situation in their times, not as settled claims about sex and gender today. Animal ethics (84) weighs arguments, never tells learners what to eat. Level 89 (machine moral status) teaches that there is no agreed answer in 2026 and makes no claim about any real product.
- **Quotations and attributions.** Quote only lines the thinker demonstrably wrote or said, from the translation named in the source; many famous "quotes" are fake (no "the unexamined life" outside Plato's Apology, no invented Confucius or Buddha sayings, no "Nietzsche said" motivational posters). Keep these straight: the trolley problem is Philippa Foot's (1967), the footbridge version Judith Jarvis Thomson's; "justified true belief" is traced to Plato's Theaetetus; "ghost in the machine" is Gilbert Ryle's mocking phrase (1949); "Cogito, ergo sum" is the Latin form in Principles of Philosophy (the Discourse has it in French; the Meditations say "I am, I exist"); "God or Nature" is Spinoza's Ethics; the problem of evil is traditionally credited to Epicurus through later writers (say "attributed to"); "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" is Beauvoir's The Second Sex (1949); Mill's higher pleasures line compares "a human being dissatisfied" with "a pig satisfied". Dates: Gettier 1963, Nagel's bat 1974, Nozick's experience machine and Wilt Chamberlain 1974, Rawls 1971, Searle 1980, Jackson 1982, Chalmers 1995, Frankfurt 1969, Turing 1950, Wollstonecraft 1792.
- **Sourcing.** Authoritative: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy first; Britannica; university philosophy department pages and open course material (such as MIT OpenCourseWare); public-domain translations of primary texts (Project Gutenberg, Perseus for Greek, the Chinese Text Project for Chinese classics) for what a thinker actually wrote. Popular quote sites, self-help and "Stoicism for success" blogs, and pop philosophy videos are never sources. Survey claims (for example, how many people would pull the switch) need a named study or the PhilPapers survey, dated; otherwise say "many people".
- **Overlaps: build on, don't repeat.** Ancient Greece owns What Is Philosophy? (51), Thales (52), Pythagoras (53), Socrates' method (54) and trial (55), Plato's Academy and the story of the cave (56), Aristotle's range (57), the Stoics and Epicureans compared (58) and Diogenes (59): this tree teaches the Forms (4), the four causes (5), the cave as an argument (71), virtue ethics (45), Epicurus on death (93) and the Stoic dichotomy of control (94), and retells none of those Greece levels. Logic & Critical Thinking owns arguments, validity, fallacies, induction as a kind of reasoning (Logic 17, 18), Occam's razor (85), the steel man (97), falsifiability and how science reasons (61 to 69): Level 26 is Hume's problem of justifying induction, not what induction is, and Level 99 must not reteach argument checks. Psychology owns Freud, behaviorism as a science and the cognitive revolution (4 to 6) and all experiments; the mind chapter is philosophy of mind, not brain science (The Human Body owns the brain, 51 to 60). How Government Works owns the social contract of Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau (7), rights, protest, unjust laws (48) and punishment (59): Chapter 6 may call back to Government 7 in a phrase and must not teach kinds of government. Art History owns Dada and Duchamp (68) and Pop Art and Warhol (82, 83): Level 92 may use Fountain or Brillo Box as a one-line case for the theories. Everyday Technology owns what AI is and how machine learning works (91); Levels 35, 36 and 89 are about thinking, understanding and moral status.
- **Near neighbours inside this tree.** Keep apart: 4 the Forms vs 71 the cave as an argument; 16 Descartes's doubt vs 24 the brain in a vat (Putnam's reply) vs 32 his dualism; 18 empiricism vs 25 a priori knowledge vs 26 induction; 12 al-Ghazali on causes vs 76 his cosmological argument; 6 Confucius's ideals vs 52 Confucians vs Legalists on rule vs 87 Confucian partiality vs Mozi; 8 the no-self argument vs 66 to 69 Western and African theories of identity; 35 the Turing test vs 36 the Chinese room vs 89 moral status; 37 Nagel vs 38 Mary vs 39 the hard problem; 46 utilitarianism vs 81 to 83 its applications; 47 the categorical imperative vs 85 lying to the murderer; 65 Sartre on freedom vs 97 Camus on meaning; 29 pragmatism vs 79 Wittgenstein.
- **Thought experiments are stated as the author set them up**, then used. Name the author and year, keep the case short enough to hold in mind, and don't add details that change the verdict. When a thought experiment has a standard set of replies (Chinese room: the systems reply; Mary: the ability hypothesis), name one.
