# Probability & Statistics: tree rules (plain numbers, honest uncertainty)

- **No formulas to memorize.** Every idea is taught in words, pictures and arithmetic a learner can do in their head or on a phone calculator (add, subtract, multiply, divide, percent). No symbols like sigma or P(A|B), no square roots, no algebra. Standard deviation, correlation, p-values and Bayes are explained as ideas, never computed. Worked examples use round, friendly numbers (10,000 people, not 9,847). Prefer counts of people ("12 out of 1,000") over percentages when explaining risk and tests.
- **Every worked number must be right.** Check each calculation twice and register derived numbers as their own claims. Classic results (two dice, the birthday problem at 23 people, Monty Hall's two in three, American roulette's 38 pockets) must match standard sources exactly; say "about" where a figure is rounded (a random sample of about 1,000 gives a margin of error of about 3 points).
- **Be honest about statistical significance.** A p-value is how surprising the data would be if there were no real effect; it is not the chance the finding is true, and "significant" does not mean "important". Follow the American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values. Don't mock science: the lesson is how to read evidence, not that studies can't be trusted.
- **Sourcing.** Authoritative: the American Statistical Association, national statistics offices (US Census Bureau, Bureau of Labor Statistics, Bureau of Justice Statistics, UK Office for National Statistics), NIST, NIH/NCI and CDC for health risk, Pew Research Center and AAPOR for polling methods, NOAA and the National Hurricane Center for forecasts, state lottery and gaming regulators for odds, and encyclopedias or university statistics departments for classic stories. Use primary papers for famous cases (Berkeley 1973 in Science, 1975; Literary Digest 1936; Lind 1747; the 1948 streptomycin trial; the Open Science Collaboration 2015). Date every changing figure (poll response rates, lottery odds, crash rates) and prefer stable ones.
- **Famous stories, told carefully.** Several classics have myths attached: say only what the record supports. The Monte Carlo 1913 run is usually given as 26 blacks. The "hot hand" is still debated (recent work finds a small real effect), so don't call it pure illusion. Sally Clark's case (Level 49) involves a real family's tragedy: state the statistical error and the court's outcome, soberly, with no speculation.
- **Gambling is taught as mathematics, never as a hobby.** Casino, lottery and betting levels show why the house wins; no tips, no systems, no sportsbook brands or promotions. Teens are in the audience.
- **Neutral on politics, health and crime.** Poll, forecast and election examples come from history (1936, 1948) or use made-up round numbers; no current candidates or parties framed as winners or losers. Health examples teach reading risk, never medical advice, and don't single out a current drug or screening program as good or bad. Crime numbers (Level 97) compare how FBI and victim-survey figures are gathered, not whether crime policy works. Algorithm levels describe the trade-off between error types as a debate between named positions.
- **Overlaps.** How Government Works Level 66 already teaches polls and margin of error, and Level 95 reading the news: build on them ("you met margin of error in Government") and go deeper into why samples work; don't contradict them. How Money Works owns compound interest, investing risk and return, diversification, inflation, CPI and unemployment measurement: use money only as examples (prices, wages, a bet) and don't re-teach those. The Human Body owns scurvy and vitamins (Level 49) and how medicines are tested (Level 96): Chapter 7 uses Lind and trials only for experiment design. Logic & Critical Thinking owns fallacies in general and correlation vs. causation as a reasoning idea; this tree owns the numbers (how strong a correlation is, how confounders distort a comparison). Psychology owns cognitive biases and how the mind judges chance: here, teach the math of the gambler's fallacy and base rates, not the psychology of why we fall for them.
- **Stay concrete and inviting.** One everyday picture per level (a pot of soup, a coin, a weather app, a doctor's test result). Numbers only when they are the point; a level about spread doesn't need five data sets.

## Who owns what in Mind & Reasoning (settled across the three trees; overrides anything above)
- **Probability & Statistics owns experimental design and every number:** observational studies vs. experiments, control groups, random assignment, placebos and blinding, significance, replication statistics, base rates and false positives, the gambler's fallacy, regression to the mean, the conjunction rule as arithmetic. Logic and Psychology mention these in a phrase at most, never as a level's concept or a question.
- **Psychology owns the experiments behind biases and heuristics:** availability, anchoring, representativeness (the Linda problem), the halo effect, Wason's 2-4-6 task, overconfidence, and the replication crisis as the history of the field. Logic never retells these experiments.
- **Logic owns reasoning as a skill:** arguments, fallacies (including appeals to nature and tradition, the perfect-solution fallacy), confirmation bias and motivated reasoning taught as habits to counter (no experiments), survivorship bias (Logic 37), Goodhart's law and incentives (Logic 49), coincidence and patterns in noise without arithmetic, how science reasons (falsifiability, hypotheses, theories, ad hoc rescues, how science changes its mind, peer review, pseudoscience), sources and decisions.
- **Elsewhere:** How Money Works owns loss aversion and behavioral economics (Money 95), opportunity cost, incentives in markets and pricing tricks; The Human Body owns the brain, senses and sleep as biology and clinical trial phases; How Government Works owns polls in elections (Government 66) and media literacy (69, 95).
- Callbacks between the three Mind trees are welcome as a phrase ("Logic's straw man"), but no question may depend on another tree.
