# Psychology: tree rules (evidence first, mental health with care)
- Teach findings at the strength the evidence supports today, and say how we know. Say "in this study" or "on average" rather than stating a single study as a law of nature. Where a famous finding failed or is contested, the level teaches that as the lesson, never as a footnote. The settled status to teach: ego depletion (Level 44) failed large multi-lab replications (2016 onward); power posing (Level 98) found no reliable hormone or behavior effects, and co-author Dana Carney disowned it in 2016, with only a small effect on how powerful people say they feel still debated; facial feedback (Level 47) is mixed (the 2016 multi-lab test failed, the 2022 "Many Smiles" study found a small effect); behavior priming like "slow walking" (Level 59) failed to replicate while word priming is solid; the marshmallow test (Level 66) shrank sharply once family background was controlled (2018); the underwater memory study (Level 34) failed a 2021 replication; the Dunning-Kruger effect (Level 58) is partly argued to be a statistical artifact. Level 55 is now the halo effect (Thorndike 1920 named it); loss aversion belongs to How Money Works 95. Don't use these as settled facts anywhere else in the tree.
- Classic studies come with their criticism. Stanford prison (Level 73): not a controlled experiment, guards were coached, and recordings and archives (Le Texier 2018) undercut the "people naturally become cruel" story. Milgram (Level 72): archive work shows varied procedures and doubting participants, yet Burger's 2009 partial replication found similar obedience. Little Albert (Level 23): one infant, never deconditioned, and his identity is disputed (don't name him as settled). Harlow (Level 61): name the ethical objections. Kitty Genovese (Level 74): the "38 silent witnesses" story was inaccurate; the bystander effect itself is real but weaker in real emergencies. Maslow (Level 42): he never drew the pyramid, and needs are not strictly ordered. Ekman (Level 46): present the universality debate (Lisa Feldman Barrett and others) as a live debate between named researchers.
- Mental health (Levels 91 to 96, and anywhere it comes up) is general education, never diagnosis or treatment advice. Use neutral, person-first, non-stigmatizing language ("a person with schizophrenia", never "crazy", "psycho", "a schizo", "committed suicide"; say "died by suicide"). No self-diagnosis checklists, no "if you have 3 of these symptoms". Never advise starting, stopping or changing medication or therapy; point to a doctor, psychologist or other licensed professional. Level 96 may give the US 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) as a resource. Follow safe messaging guidelines: no self-harm or suicide method detail, no numbers of deaths by method, no description of means, no eating-disorder weights or calorie counts. Don't say depression is "a chemical imbalance"; say the serotonin-only explanation is now seen as too simple, while antidepressants help many people.
- Sourcing: APA (including the APA Dictionary of Psychology), NIMH, NHS, WHO, SAMHSA and the CDC for mental health; the DSM-5-TR as described by the APA (American Psychiatric Association); OpenStax Psychology 2e, Britannica and university psychology departments for concepts and history; the original papers (via journal pages, PubMed or PMC) and the published replication projects (Open Science Collaboration 2015 in Science, the Many Labs projects, Registered Replication Reports) for findings; Cochrane reviews for treatment evidence. Pop-psychology sites, TED talks and self-help books are never sources. Every study named gets its year and lead researcher right.
- Numbers from studies are the study's numbers, dated and hedged ("about 65 percent of participants in Milgram's best-known version"). Habit formation: Lally and colleagues (2010) found a median of about 66 days, with a wide range (18 to 254), not "21 days". Working memory: Miller's "seven, plus or minus two" (1956) is now usually revised to about four chunks (Cowan). Don't teach statistics (p-values, effect sizes, base rates) in depth: describe them in words and leave the math to Probability & Statistics.
- Overlaps: The Human Body owns the brain and nerves as biology (51 to 60, including 55 left brain/right brain, 56 how memories are made and why we forget, 57 sleep, 58 why we dream, 59 hormones), the senses and optical illusions (61 to 70), twins (83), the body clock (89), sleep and health (93), stress on the body (94) and the 10 percent myth (99). Build on them in a phrase ("your ear turns sound into signals"); don't reteach them, and don't contradict them. Psychology's angle is behavior and experience: what people notice, remember, feel and do, and the experiments that showed it.
- More overlaps: Logic & Critical Thinking owns fallacies and reasoning errors as logic; this tree owns the experiments on biases (Chapter 6 teaches the research, such as Wason's 2-4-6 task, not rules of argument). Probability & Statistics owns the numbers behind studies and the conjunction rule (Level 54 teaches representativeness as a judgment shortcut, not probability math). How Money Works owns behavioral economics as money (16 price nudges, 89 advertising, 95 behavioral economics); keep Chapter 6 on the psychology experiments. Music owns music and the brain and the "Mozart effect" myth; How Government Works owns misinformation and polls. Everyday Technology's "working memory" is computer memory; don't mix the two.
- Stay neutral and humane: examples from many cultures, not only the US, and no mocking of people who fell for biases or of study participants. Development and aging levels avoid stereotypes (older adults are not frail by default; teenagers are not "broken"). Personality and IQ (Levels 81 to 84) describe measures and what they predict for groups, never rank groups of people; don't discuss group differences in IQ. Parenting and attachment levels never blame parents.
- Level art: no brains drawn with labels, no faces of real researchers, no people in distress, and no letters or numbers on any object (blank cards, blank dials).

## Who owns what in Mind & Reasoning (settled across the three trees; overrides anything above)
- **Probability & Statistics owns experimental design and every number:** observational studies vs. experiments, control groups, random assignment, placebos and blinding, significance, replication statistics, base rates and false positives, the gambler's fallacy, regression to the mean, the conjunction rule as arithmetic. Logic and Psychology mention these in a phrase at most, never as a level's concept or a question.
- **Psychology owns the experiments behind biases and heuristics:** availability, anchoring, representativeness (the Linda problem), the halo effect, Wason's 2-4-6 task, overconfidence, and the replication crisis as the history of the field. Logic never retells these experiments.
- **Logic owns reasoning as a skill:** arguments, fallacies (including appeals to nature and tradition, the perfect-solution fallacy), confirmation bias and motivated reasoning taught as habits to counter (no experiments), survivorship bias (Logic 37), Goodhart's law and incentives (Logic 49), coincidence and patterns in noise without arithmetic, how science reasons (falsifiability, hypotheses, theories, ad hoc rescues, how science changes its mind, peer review, pseudoscience), sources and decisions.
- **Elsewhere:** How Money Works owns loss aversion and behavioral economics (Money 95), opportunity cost, incentives in markets and pricing tricks; The Human Body owns the brain, senses and sleep as biology and clinical trial phases; How Government Works owns polls in elections (Government 66) and media literacy (69, 95).
- Callbacks between the three Mind trees are welcome as a phrase ("Logic's straw man"), but no question may depend on another tree.
