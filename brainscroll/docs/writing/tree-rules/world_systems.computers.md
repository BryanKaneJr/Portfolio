# Computers & the Internet: tree rules (plain language, no hype, no brand cheerleading)

- **No code required.** Every idea is taught in words, everyday pictures and arithmetic a learner can do in their head. No code listings, no syntax to memorize; a keyword like `if` or a tiny made-up instruction is fine only when the level is about it (Levels 21 to 24). Binary appears as small worked examples (a byte, adding two small numbers), never long conversions. Explain jargon the first time it appears in a level (IP, DNS, TCP, HTTPS, GDPR).
- **Be exact about the easy-to-garble facts.** A byte is 8 bits with 256 patterns; internet speeds are sold in megabits per second, file sizes in megabytes; a "1 TB" drive holds 10^12 bytes, which an operating system counting in powers of 1,024 shows as about 931 GB. The internet (the network) is not the web (pages on it). Encryption, hashing and encoding are three different things: hashing is one-way, encryption is reversible with a key, encoding (ASCII, Unicode) hides nothing. HTTPS proves you reached the site the certificate names, not that the site is honest.
- **History, told carefully.** Keep to what the record supports and give years, not myths: Jacquard's loom (about 1804), Hollerith's 1890 census, Lovelace's notes (1843), Turing (1936), ENIAC (completed 1945, unveiled 1946) and its six women programmers, the Manchester Baby (1948), the transistor (Bell Labs, 1947), Moore's prediction (1965, revised to every two years in 1975), the Altair (1975), Apple II (1977), IBM PC (1981), the iPhone (2007). The 1947 moth was a bug found in the Harvard Mark II and logged as the "first actual case of bug being found": the word "bug" was already in use, so don't say the moth coined it. Credit teams, not lone geniuses, where that is the truth (the transistor, the integrated circuit's two inventors, Kilby and Noyce).
- **AI as of 2026, honestly.** Describe large language models as systems trained to predict the next token on vast text, then fine-tuned with human feedback; say they can be very capable and still state falsehoods fluently. No claims that they "understand" or "think" like people, and no claims that they are "just autocomplete" with nothing more to say: present the debate as a debate. Any statement about current abilities, costs or energy use is dated ("as of 2025") and sourced; say "about" for estimates. Never rank or promote AI companies or products (including Anthropic's); name products only as historical examples (ChatGPT's 2022 launch) or when a level needs one.
- **Neutral on companies, governments and live debates.** No brand cheerleading or bashing; outages, breaches and fines are stated as the documented facts (the October 2021 Facebook outage, the 2017 Equifax breach) with no editorializing. Contested policy questions (end-to-end encryption and lawful access, privacy laws, content moderation, platform power, AI regulation, open vs. closed AI models) are presented as views of named sides (privacy groups, law enforcement agencies, companies, regulators), never the app's view.
- **Security levels teach defense only.** Explain how attacks work in principle (phishing, password guessing, ransomware) so learners can protect themselves; no step-by-step attack instructions, no real malware names that teach technique, no tools for cracking. Advice follows NIST SP 800-63B and CISA (long passphrases, a password manager, multi-factor authentication or passkeys, prompt updates, backups). Level 92 ("Is my phone listening?") reports what studies found (for example the 2018 Northeastern University study of more than 17,000 Android apps, which found no secret audio recording but did find apps sending screen recordings) and the simpler explanations for eerie ads; don't assert more than the evidence shows either way.
- **Sourcing.** Authoritative: NIST, CISA and the UK National Cyber Security Centre for security; IETF RFCs, ICANN, IANA and the regional internet registries for how the internet works; the IEA and US DOE/LBNL for data-center energy (date every figure); the Computer History Museum, the Smithsonian, the Science Museum (London) and Britannica for history; primary papers for landmark results (Turing 1936, Moore 1965, Sweeney's re-identification work, Ouyang et al. 2022 on fine-tuning with human feedback); official texts for laws (the EU's GDPR, the California Consumer Privacy Act). Company blogs are fine for a company's own outage report, never for claims about rivals or about how good their product is.
- **Overlaps: go deeper, don't repeat.** Everyday Technology already teaches What a Computer Is (51), Binary (52), the Microchip (53), Memory and Storage (54), Software and Code (55), Algorithms with sorting (56), the First Computers (57), From Rooms to Pockets (58), Operating Systems (59), How the Internet Began, Packets, Undersea Cables, Wi-Fi, the Web, Search Engines, the Cloud, Encryption, Passwords and Hacking (61 to 69), Cell Phones and What's Inside a Smartphone (48, 49), Artificial Intelligence (91), How Chatbots Work (92) and Quantum Computers (97). Treat those as known ("you met binary in Everyday Technology") and teach the next layer down (gates, the processor cycle, DNS, TCP, hashing, public keys, tokens). Never re-teach ARPANET, sorting, the basic internet vs. web distinction or quantum bits as a level's concept, and don't contradict them.
- **Other neighbours.** Probability & Statistics owns big data's pitfalls (91), A/B tests (92) and the false-alarm vs. miss trade-off and data bias in prediction algorithms (95): Levels 79 and 81 to 82 may mention them in a phrase, not teach them. Logic owns judging sources, echo chambers and out-of-context photos (81 to 90): Levels 89, 92 and 99 stay on the technology (how synthetic media is made, what a phone can technically record, how a tech claim can be checked). Psychology owns attention, habits and screen-time effects: no level on whether screens are bad for you. How Money Works owns cryptocurrency (9) and scams as money (19): Level 59 teaches phishing as a security technique, not fraud in general. Government owns the right to privacy (36) and misinformation (69): Level 69 here covers what privacy laws let you do with your data, not privacy as a constitutional right. Chemistry owns why silicon is a semiconductor (39).
- **Things writers get wrong.** "The cloud" is other people's computers, not something in the sky. Incognito mode hides history from others on your device, not from websites or your internet provider. Deleting a file usually removes its entry, not its data (Level 28), but SSDs often erase deleted blocks automatically, so say "often" recoverable. A VPN moves trust to the VPN company; it doesn't make you anonymous. Moore's law is an observation about industry, not a law of physics. Quantum computers threaten today's public-key encryption (RSA and elliptic curves), not all encryption; NIST published its first post-quantum standards in August 2024.
